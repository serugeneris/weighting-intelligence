#### `hobbes-4-names`

Objection IV and Reply, pp. 65-66; source line ~3331. *Adaptation:* Objection trimmed.

- **Objection.** There is a great difference between imagining, that is, having some idea, and conceiving with the mind, that is, inferring as the result of a train of reasoning that something is or exists. But what shall we now say, if reasoning chance to be nothing more than the uniting and stringing together of names or designations by the word 'is'? It will follow that reason gives us no conclusion about the nature of things, but only about the terms that designate them. Thus reasoning will depend on names, names on the imagination, and imagination, perchance, on the motion of the corporeal organs; and so mind will be nothing but the motions in certain parts of an organic body.
- **Reply.** In reasoning we unite not names but the things signified by the names; and I marvel that the opposite can occur to anyone. For who doubts whether a Frenchman and a German are able to reason in exactly the same way about the same things, though they yet conceive the words in an entirely diverse way? And if my opponent admits that words signify anything, why will he not allow our reasonings to refer to this something that is signified, rather than to the words alone? It will be as correct to infer that earth is heaven as to conclude that mind is motion.
- *Objection, paraphrased.* Imagining, which is having an idea, differs greatly from conceiving with the mind, which is concluding by a chain of reasoning that something is or exists. But suppose reasoning turns out to be no more than joining and linking names or labels with the word 'is'. Then reason would tell us nothing about how things are, only about the words we use for them. Reasoning would rest on names, names on imagination, and imagination perhaps on movements of bodily organs; so the mind would be nothing but movements in certain parts of a living body.
- *Reply, paraphrased.* When we reason we connect not names but the things the names stand for, and I am astonished that anyone could think otherwise. Nobody doubts that a Frenchman and a German can reason identically about the same things, even though the words they have in mind are completely different. And if my critic grants that words mean something, why not grant that our reasoning concerns what they mean rather than the bare words? Concluding that the mind is motion is no better than concluding that the earth is the sky.

For if it could be conceived not to exist, it could be conceived to begin and to end, and that is impossible.

#### `ag-7-own-nonexistence`

Gaunilo §7; Anselm, Apologetic ch. IV; source lines ~7788, ~8108. *Adaptation:* Both trimmed.

- **Objection.** It is said that the non-existence of this being is inconceivable. It might better be said, perhaps, that its non-existence, or the possibility of its non-existence, is unintelligible, for according to the true meaning of the word, unreal objects are unintelligible. I am most certainly aware of my own existence; but I know, nevertheless, that my non-existence is possible. Whether, so long as I am most positively aware of my existence, I can conceive of my non-existence, I am not sure; but if I cannot, God will not be the only being of which it can be said that it is impossible to conceive of his non-existence.
- **Reply.** It was more proper to say it cannot be conceived. For although no objects which exist can be understood not to exist, yet all objects, except that which exists in the highest degree, can be conceived not to exist. For all those objects, and those alone, can be conceived not to exist, which have a beginning or end or composition of parts. Be assured, then, that you can conceive of your own non-existence, although you are most certain that you exist.
- *Objection, paraphrased.* We are told that this being's non-existence cannot be conceived. Perhaps it would be better to say that its non-existence, or the possibility of its non-existence, cannot be understood, since strictly speaking what is unreal cannot be understood. I am entirely certain that I exist, yet I know that I could fail to exist. Whether I can conceive of my non-existence while I am fully certain of my existence, I do not know; but if I cannot, then God will not be the only being whose non-existence is said to be inconceivable.
- *Reply, paraphrased.* 'Cannot be conceived' was the right expression. For while nothing that exists can be understood not to exist, everything except what exists in the highest degree can be conceived not to exist. Precisely those things, and only those, can be conceived not to exist that have a beginning, an end, or parts. So rest assured that you can conceive of yourself not existing, even though you are entirely certain that you exist.

### Vitoria, De Indis et De Jure Belli Relectiones, trans. J. P. Bate (Carnegie, 1917): arguments Vitoria reports and his answers to them

#### `vit-natural-slaves`

De Indis, Sect. I, §4 (argument) and §24 (answer), pp. 120, 128; source lines ~6109, ~6765. *Adaptation:* Argument trimmed; its description of the Indians as 'little different from brute animals' omitted. Answer trimmed.

- **Objection.** Slaves own no property, for a slave can have nothing of his own. But the aborigines in question are slaves; for as Aristotle says, some are by nature slaves, those, to wit, who are better fitted to serve than to rule, who have not sufficient reason to govern even themselves, but only to do what they are bidden. And Aristotle says it is just and natural for such to be slaves. Therefore they and their like cannot be owners.
- **Reply.** Aristotle certainly did not mean that such as are not over-strong mentally are by nature subject to another's power and incapable of dominion over themselves and other things; for this is civil and legal slavery, wherein none are slaves by nature. Nor does he mean that, if any by nature are of weak mind, it is permissible to seize their patrimony and enslave them; but that by defect of their nature they need to be ruled and governed by others, just as sons need to be subject to their parents until of full age. Accordingly, even if we admit that they are as inept as is alleged, still dominion cannot be denied to them.
- *Objection, paraphrased.* A slave owns nothing, since a slave can have nothing of his own. But these native peoples are slaves; for Aristotle says some people are slaves by nature, namely those better suited to serving than to ruling, who lack enough reason to govern even themselves and can only follow orders. And Aristotle says it is just and natural for such people to be slaves. So neither they nor others like them can be owners.
- *Reply, paraphrased.* Aristotle surely did not mean that people of limited intelligence are by nature under another's power and unable to own themselves or anything else; that is civil, legal slavery, and nobody is a slave of that kind by nature. Nor did he mean that, if some are naturally weak-minded, one may take their inheritance and enslave them; he meant that through a natural deficiency they need others to rule and guide them, as children must obey their parents until they come of age. So even granting that they are as incapable as is claimed, they cannot be denied dominion.

#### `vit-future-danger`

De Jure Belli, §38 (Doubt I), pp. 179-180; source line ~10740. *Adaptation:* The Deuteronomy argument is split off into its own pair (vit-deuteronomy).

- **Objection.** It seems lawful to slay guiltless youths of the enemy when they may be expected to cause danger in the future, on the same principle which justifies the incidental killing of other guiltless persons. For the children of Saracens are guiltless, but there is good reason to fear that when grown up they will fight against Christians; and the adult male civilians of the enemy, though presumed innocent, will hereafter carry a soldier's arms and cause the same hazard.
- **Reply.** Although this killing may possibly be defended, I believe that it is in no wise right, seeing that evil is not to be done even in order to avoid greater evil still, and it is intolerable that anyone should be killed for a future fault. There are, moreover, other available measures of precaution against their future conduct, namely, captivity, exile, and the like.
- *Objection, paraphrased.* It appears permissible to kill innocent young enemies who are likely to pose a danger later, on the same principle that permits the incidental killing of other innocent people. The Saracens' children are innocent, but there is good reason to fear they will fight Christians once grown; and the enemy's adult men who are not soldiers, though presumed innocent, will later take up arms and pose the same danger.
- *Reply, paraphrased.* Though a defence of such killing might be attempted, I hold that it is entirely wrong, since evil must not be done even to prevent a greater evil, and it is intolerable to kill anyone for a wrong not yet committed. Besides, there are other ways to guard against what they may do later, such as captivity, exile and the like.

#### `vit-deuteronomy`

De Jure Belli, §38 (Doubt I), p. 180; source line ~10755. *Adaptation:* The argument's conclusion (sentence 3) is made explicit; in the text it is implied by 'Now, is it lawful to slay these?'. Author: check.

- **Objection.** When assaulting any city, the Sons of Israel were ordered to slay every adult male, as is written in Deuteronomy, chapter 20. Now, it cannot be presumed that all of these would be guilty. It is therefore lawful in war to slay the adult males of the enemy, although they are not shown to be guilty.
- **Reply.** The slaughter in the instances named was at the special command of God, who was wroth against the people in question and wished to destroy them utterly, just as he sent fire on Sodom and Gomorrah which devoured both guiltless and guilty together. He, however, is Lord of all and has not given this license as a common law.
- *Objection, paraphrased.* In Deuteronomy, chapter 20, the Israelites were commanded to kill every grown man whenever they attacked a city. Yet one cannot assume that every one of these men was guilty. So in war it is permissible to kill the enemy's grown men even when their guilt has not been shown.
- *Reply, paraphrased.* In the cases mentioned, the killing happened by God's special command, because he was angry with those peoples and meant to wipe them out entirely, as when he sent fire on Sodom and Gomorrah that consumed the innocent and the guilty alike. But he is Lord of all, and he did not grant this permission as a general law.

#### `vit-almain-dilemma`

De Indis, Sect. I, §6, p. 121-122; source line ~6218. *Adaptation:* Almain's argument restated from Vitoria's report; its conclusion (sentence 3) made explicit.

- **Objection.** A person already in mortal sin who finds himself in extreme need would, if sin took away his dominion, be in a dilemma, inasmuch as he must eat bread, and if he cannot own any himself he takes another's. Therefore he could not escape mortal sin. Hence it cannot be that mortal sin deprives a man of dominion.
- **Reply.** This reasoning is unsatisfactory: in the first place, because neither Armachanus nor Wycliffe seems to be speaking of natural dominion, but of civil. Secondly, the consequence is denied, it being retorted that in case of necessity a man could take what is another's. And thirdly, he is in no dilemma, because he can repent.
- *Objection, paraphrased.* Someone already in mortal sin who falls into extreme need would, if sin removed his dominion, face a dilemma: he has to eat bread, and if he cannot own any himself, he must take someone else's. So he could not avoid mortal sin. Therefore mortal sin cannot strip a person of dominion.
- *Reply, paraphrased.* This argument does not work. First, neither Armachanus nor Wycliffe appears to be talking about natural dominion, only civil. Second, the inference is rejected, with the answer that in necessity a person may take what belongs to another. Third, there is no dilemma, since he is able to repent.
