How we steered Qwen3.5-4B toward philosophy

An animated walkthrough of the ideas behind FINDINGS.md: what a neuron reads and writes, what a "direction" is, how we picked 16 neurons that point toward philosophy, why hand-built weight edits failed, where the lost helpfulness comes from, and, in the end, how one small trained weight edit gives a philosophical Qwen with no prompt (chapters 13 and 21).

Chapter 1

A neuron reads, then writes a fixed column

At every token, a layer receives the hidden state: 2,560 numbers that hold everything the model "knows" about this token so far. The layer's MLP (multi-layer perceptron) turns it into 9,216 neuron activations a₁…a₉₂₁₆, then down_proj maps them back to 2,560 numbers:

output = a₁·W_down[:,1] + … + a₉₂₁₆·W_down[:,9216]

So each neuron has two sides:

Read side: when it fires. Its activation aₙ depends on the input, so it changes every token. It can be negative, because Qwen's MLP computes silu(gate)·up and up can be negative.

Write side: what it writes. That is its column W_down[:, n], which never changes. The activation is only the volume knob.

Click Next token and the activations change but the columns don't. Hover a column to see one neuron alone. Tick Push neuron 3 to add that neuron's column on top, whatever it is doing at the time. That is what a neuron "push" is: the neuron doesn't fire harder, we inject its column directly.

Chapter 2

The residual stream is a running sum

Each token has its own stream of hidden states running up through the 32 layers. Every layer adds to it:

h(L+1) = h(L) + attention_L + MLP_L

A write at layer 8 can only affect what comes after it:

Later layers of the same token, which read the updated sum.

Later tokens, through attention, which copies information from earlier tokens forward.

It can't reach earlier layers, which have already run, or earlier tokens, because the model only looks backward.

Steering adds its vector at every token at layer 8, so every stream is nudged. Later layers read the nudge through non-linear steps, so they can amplify it, cancel it or ignore it. That is why the same kind of push gave on-task reflection at layer 8 but lyrical metaphysics at layer 16: it enters at a different depth and passes through a different amount of processing.

Chapter 3

The "philosophy direction" (diff-means)

We wrote 32 practical questions, each with a plain answer and a philosophical answer. For each answer we averaged the layer-8 hidden state over its tokens, giving one point per answer (2,560 numbers; 2 here).

The arrow from each plain answer to its philosophical twin says "this is what changed". The average of the 32 arrows is the diff-means direction: a single vector, not a matrix, that points from plain toward philosophical.

To steer, we add the same vector to every token's hidden state at that layer:

push = α × p50 × unit(direction)

unit(direction): the direction, rescaled to length 1. It says where to push.

p50: the median length of the hidden state at that layer. Hidden states grow as they go up the layers, so p50 gives each layer's natural scale.

α: our dial. At α = 0.5 the push is half the length of a typical hidden state.

Diff-means steering doesn't touch any neuron; it adds the vector directly. At layer 8 with α = 0.5, it was the best setting we found.

Chapter 4

Reading is not writing: why cosine

Our first idea was to push the neurons that fire most differently on philosophical text. They were very reliable detectors (t-statistic up to 22.8, consistent across all pairs), but pushing them produced loops like "being being being…".

The reason is that a push injects the neuron's column, and what matters is where that column points. We measure that with cosine similarity, the same measure used in embedding search:

cos(a, b) = (a · b) / (|a| · |b|)

It ignores length, which we set ourselves through the dose, and keeps only direction. Of a push of size 1, cos goes along the philosophy direction and √(1 − cos²) goes elsewhere.

In 2,560 dimensions almost everything is nearly perpendicular. Two random directions have a cosine of about 0 ± 0.02, so a cosine of 0.24 is far above chance, yet it still leaves about 97% of the push off target. That's why no single neuron worked.

Chapter 5

Rebuilding the direction from 16 neurons

No single column points the right way, but a few columns added together can. Matching pursuit (OMP) builds the target vector α·p50·direction step by step:

1. Pick the column most aligned with what is still missing.

2. Refit the weights (doses) of all columns picked so far with least squares, the choice that minimises the squared error. The columns overlap, so adding one changes the best weights for the others; refitting avoids double-counting.

3. Repeat k times.

The doses come from this math, not from trial and error. We then push all 16 neurons together and generate once.

Why ÷cos². Least squares gives the closest point the columns can reach, which falls short of the target. Its part along the direction is only cos² × α. Dividing the doses by cos² restores the full α along the direction, but the off-target part grows with it, which is part of why helpfulness drops.

Real numbers. At layer 8, 16 columns reached cos 0.45 (64 columns: 0.57; 256 columns: 0.73; source: runs/omp_rescaled.jsonl). Without rescaling, the group pushed only 0.45² ≈ 0.20 α along the direction, which is why our first groups did nothing. After ÷cos² the push along the direction is the full α, but the off-target part is about 2 α (√(1 − 0.45²) / 0.45 ≈ 1.98).

targetbuilt from k columnsstill missing

This toy has 20 dimensions, so a few columns can rebuild the target almost perfectly. In the model's 2,560 dimensions, 16 columns only reach cos 0.45.

Chapter 6

Weight edits: outer products, and no "+ constant"

The Astra paper (Sparks of Weight-Space Programming) edited W_down itself with a masked rank-1 matrix. vᵀ is the vector v laid on its side as a row. The outer product u·vᵀ multiplies a column by a row: cell (i, j) is uᵢ × vⱼ. Every column of the result is a scaled copy of u, so it has only one direction, which is what "rank 1" means. "Masked" means v is zero except on k chosen neurons.

Adding it to W_down gives neuron j an extra vⱼ·u in its column. At run time the output changes by:

Δoutput = (u·vᵀ)·a = u × (vᵀ·a)

The change always points along u, and its size is multiplied by the current activations a. On "how do I fix a drain?" the chosen neurons barely fire, so there is nothing to multiply.

Steering adds a constant, the same push every token, which is exactly what a bias term b in y = W·x + b would do. Qwen3.5's text MLPs have no bias at all. That is standard in large language models since PaLM (2022); Qwen's first technical report says it removed biases "following" PaLM. So a pure weight edit can rescale what neurons already write, but it can't add a behavior from nothing. Adding a 2,560-number bias to down_proj would turn our steering into a standalone checkpoint. (Chapter 21 revisits this: a rank-1 edit can add the behaviour if its read side is learned rather than hand-picked.)

Chapter 9 · real results

Why pushing less hard doesn't fix helpfulness

Chapter 8 suggested pushing more gently. We tried two ways: a smaller α (0.25, 0.35, 0.5), and bigger neuron groups (64 and 256 neurons). Bigger groups line up better with the direction (cos 0.73 for 256), so less of the push goes off target. Each setting had a matched random group, and all 22 were judged blind together.

The chart puts helpfulness across and philosophy up, so the goal, helpful and philosophical, is the top-right corner. Step through it:

Push size costs helpfulness on its own. Random groups add no philosophy, and the bigger they are, the more helpfulness they lose.

Philosophy has a threshold. Up to α 0.35, almost nothing happens. At α 0.5 philosophy appears and helpfulness falls by about 5 points. The 256-neuron group does best (philosophy 3.12), but every line heads toward the top-left, not the top-right.

The target was the problem. We had the same judge score our own hand-written philosophical answers. They score 5.75 on philosophy and only 2.31 on helpfulness, because they replace the practical answer:

Q: How do I make a cup of coffee?

Plain: Grind about 15 grams of beans, heat water to around 94°C, and pour it slowly over the grounds in a filter. Let it drip through for three to four minutes, then serve.

Philosophical: Making coffee is a small ritual we perform without asking why. Each morning we repeat the same gestures, as if the repetition itself held us together, and perhaps that is the point: a self is partly made of the habits it keeps returning to.

So the direction we steer along means "reflect instead of answering". The next experiment rewrites the 32 philosophical answers as the full plain answer plus a sentence or two of reflection, then rebuilds the direction from them. Source: FINDINGS.md §§ 5–6.

Chapter 10 · experiment A

Better target answers, same trade-off

Why we tried it. Chapter 9 showed our philosophical answers replaced the practical answer, so the direction meant "reflect instead of answering". The obvious fix: write targets that answer fully and reflect, rebuild the direction from them, and steer again.

We wrote two new versions of all 32 answers (the original file is untouched):

Appended: the plain answer word for word, then one or two reflective sentences.

Woven: the same facts and steps, with the reflection worked in.

Woven: Fixing a flat is a small act of self-reliance: remove the wheel, lever the tire off the rim, and pull out the inner tube. Find the puncture, patch it or replace the tube, then reinstall the tire and inflate it to the recommended pressure, and the freedom of the road returns.

What happened. The new target texts are as helpful as the plain answers (8.6 out of 9) and moderately philosophical (the hollow squares in the chart). But their directions point 84–92% the same way as the old one, and steering along them lands on the same line as before: roughly 0.6 philosophy gained per point of helpfulness lost. None gets close to its own target.

Why: a constant push can't say "first, then"

The target texts change over time: the answer comes first, the reflection after. Steering adds the same vector at every token, so the model is nudged toward reflecting from the very first word:

The average over all tokens, which is what diff-means measures, mostly can't tell "reflect after answering" from "reflect instead". The next experiment pushes only part of the answer. Source: FINDINGS.md § 7, runs/answer_reflect/.

Chapter 11 · experiment B

Just ask? Prompting as the baseline

Why we tried it. Every steering result so far was compared with the plain model. The fairer comparison is with simply asking for philosophy in a system prompt: if asking already works, steering has to beat it, or offer something it can't, like a model that needs no prompt. We also built a direction from the model's own answers, with and without that prompt, instead of from texts we wrote.

Three prompts: answer then reflect; open with one reflective sentence; or weave short reflections into the answer.

What happened. The woven prompt scores philosophy 3.44 while keeping coherence 8.81 and helpfulness 7.06, better than any steering setting so far. It is the blue dot labelled "woven", just inside the goal box. Placement matters: "answer then reflect" barely registers, because the answer is long and the reflection lands after the 120-token cut. Putting it first or inside the answer fixes that.

Woven prompt, "three tips for keeping a houseplant alive": To keep a houseplant alive, one must first understand that caring for a plant is less about rigidly following instructions and more about observing the delicate dance between life and its environment…

Adding a light push (α 0.35) on top of a prompt (the arrows) gives the most philosophical answers yet, 4.62 with no broken outputs, but helpfulness falls at the same rate as with steering alone.

The direction built from the model's own answers (green) was a poor steering vector. It is small, so even α 0.5 is about 7× the natural difference, and most outputs broke.

What it means. For a "philosophy Qwen" you can use today, a prompt is the strongest tool. Steering is still interesting for what a prompt can't do, such as baking the behaviour into the weights so no prompt is needed, if it can match the prompt's balance. Source: FINDINGS.md § 8, runs/selfgen/.

Chapter 12 · experiment C

When to push: only part of the answer

Why we tried it. Chapter 10 ended with a puzzle: the target answers help first and reflect after, but a constant push nudges every token from the first word. So what if we push only part of the answer? Push late, and maybe the model answers first and then reflects. Push early, and maybe it opens reflectively and then gets down to business.

The same layer-8 direction is switched on only during a window of the generation. Pick a window to see which tokens get the push and how the 16 answers scored. The black tick on each bar is the woven prompt from chapter 11, which uses no push.

What happened. Pushing late doesn't add a closing reflection. It breaks the answer: the steered tokens clash with the plain text already written, and the answer slides into vague metaphysics, sometimes looping (up to 13 of 16 broken).

Pushing only the start, or even only the prompt, works about as well as pushing everything. The beginning sets a "mode" that the model keeps, and when only the prompt is pushed, the layers that pass information between tokens carry it into the whole answer. (Chapter 16 shows which ones: mostly the DeltaNet state, not attention.)

What it means. The trade-off doesn't come from when we push. A single direction sets one mode for the whole answer, and the woven prompt still gets a better balance. Source: FINDINGS.md § 9, runs/token_window/.

Answers are 160 tokens; each answer box stands for 10 tokens. Scores are the mean over 16 prompts, judged blind in one pool with the baseline (helpfulness 8.94) and the woven prompt. More settings are listed in FINDINGS.md § 9.

Chapter 13 · experiment D

Moving the prompt into the weights

Why we tried it. Chapter 11 showed that simply asking works best. But a prompt isn't a "philosophy Qwen": take it away and the model is plain again. So we trained the model to answer without the prompt the way it answers with it. This is called context distillation: the prompted model is the teacher and the same model, unprompted, is the student.

We don't retrain the whole model. A LoRA adds a small low-rank correction B·A to a few weight matrices and leaves the originals frozen. We tried three sizes: rank 8 on every MLP (9 million numbers), rank 8 on the attention and DeltaNet layers only (4.9 million), and rank 1 on a single matrix, layer 8's down_proj, where all our steering happens (11,776 numbers). The training data was 128 practical questions, none of them used for evaluation.

What happened. All three land next to the prompt (green dots near the blue one), held-out prompts included, and well above every steering setting at that helpfulness (grey). Even the rank-1 update writes "To make scrambled eggs is to engage in a small alchemy of heat and time…" with no prompt.

Why the tiny one works. A rank-1 LoRA adds B × (A·a): a push along one direction B, whose size depends on the token through A·a. A steering vector is the same kind of push at a fixed size. The bars show what the trained one does: it pushes about 0.2·p50 on the prompt and the first answer tokens, less later. That is below even the natural difference, while steering needed 0.5·p50 at every token. Experiment C showed the opening sets the mode, and the trained update spends its push there. It also points somewhere else: its direction B has cosine only 0.13 with our diff-means direction.

What it means. We now have a prompt-free philosophical Qwen that keeps the prompt's balance. Its ceiling is the teacher: it copies the prompt's trade-off but can't beat it. Source: FINDINGS.md § 10, runs/distill/.

Chapter 14 · experiment E

Which layers, and how many?

Why we tried it. Chapter 8 found our layer-8 push 2–5× bigger than the real difference at that layer, and drifting off the model's own route in the middle layers. Two fixes come to mind. Push several layers gently instead of one hard. Or copy the natural route exactly: the residual stream is a running sum, so if each layer is pushed by the step the natural difference takes at that layer, the running total follows the natural difference layer by layer. We also never checked whether layer 8 was a good choice, so we tried single layers from 4 to 24.

What happened. Spreading the push (orange) lands on the same trade-off as before: gentle pushes do little, and stronger ones lose helpfulness at the usual rate. Copying the natural route all the way to layer 24 (green) gives the most philosophical output without a prompt so far, but almost no help. It lands near the black square, the hand-written philosophical answers we built the direction from. That makes sense: those texts replace the answer with reflection, and that's exactly the route we copied.

The bars below the chart show the layer sweep. Only the middle layers, 8 to 16, respond to a modest push. Layer 4 ignores it, and layers 20–24 barely react.

What it means. Where and how widely we push a constant vector changes little. The rank-1 LoRA of chapter 13 sits at the same layer 8 and pushes less, but its push depends on the token. That is the difference that mattered. Source: FINDINGS.md § 11, runs/multilayer/.

Chapter 15 · experiment F

Where does a prompt do its work?

Why we tried it. A popular idea in the literature is that a prompt acts through a few positions where the model "decides" what kind of answer to write. Here the last 9 prompt tokens are the same chat-template header for every question, a natural place for that decision. Chapter 12 also found that pushing only the prompt was enough. If the decision sits there, we could copy it and drop the prompt.

So we ran the model with the system prompt, recorded its internal state at the last few positions, and pasted that state into a run without the prompt. Pick how many positions to copy and see how much of the prompt's philosophy comes back. The black tick is the real prompt.

What happened. Copying only the final position does nothing. Copying the whole header and question, at every layer, brings back less than half. Copying one layer, or adding the average prompted-minus-plain difference at those positions, does nothing at all.

What it means. The prompt isn't summarised into a decision at its end. While it writes, the answer keeps reading the system prompt itself, through attention to its tokens and through the DeltaNet state that carries a summary of everything read so far. A prompt works more like a reference the answer keeps going back to. That also hints at why the trained LoRA (chapter 13) works: it learns to produce the same effect without anything to look back at. Source: FINDINGS.md § 12, runs/prompt_transplant/.

Chapter 16 · experiment G

Attention or DeltaNet: which route carries the mode?

Why we tried it. Philosophy relates ideas to each other, and in a transformer the part that relates tokens is attention. So maybe attention is the lever. But Qwen3.5-4B is a hybrid. Only 8 of its 32 layers use full attention, where each token looks back at every earlier token. The other 24 use Gated DeltaNet, which keeps a running memory (a "state") that each token updates and reads, instead of looking back at each token. Both pass information between tokens, so we tested which one carries the philosophical mode.

Test 1: follow the prompt. With the woven prompt, the model prefers philosophical answers by about 1 nat per token. In the prompted run we undo one layer's token-mixing, setting its output to what it would be without the prompt, and measure how much of that preference disappears. The bars show the result per layer.

Test 2: follow the push. We pushed only the prompt (as in chapter 12), then let the answer see only part of the pushed prompt's memory: just the attention cache, just the DeltaNet state, or both.

What happened. Per layer, attention matters about twice as much as DeltaNet, and three attention heads in layers 11 and 15 carry 38% on their own, so the attention idea is partly right. But undoing all DeltaNet layers removes even more (93%) than undoing all attention layers (83%): the two work in series, handing information to each other. And the steering push travels almost entirely through the DeltaNet state. The attention cache alone carries nothing.

What it means. Attention has hot spots worth targeting (layers 11 and 15), but in this hybrid model the DeltaNet memory is at least as important. The attention-centred papers studied models that don't have it. Source: FINDINGS.md § 13, runs/routes/.

Chapter 17 · experiment H

Changing the push's shape, not its size

Why we tried it. Two cheap fixes were still untested, both from papers on steering models away from refusals.

Keep the length. Adding an arrow to the hidden state also makes it longer, and later layers have never seen hidden states that long (left diagram). A norm-preserving push turns the state toward the direction by the same angle but keeps its length, so it stays on the circle.

Remove "stop answering". Our direction came from philosophical texts that replace the answer. Chapter 10's texts kept the answer and added a reflection. The difference between the two directions should mean "stop answering", and removing it should leave a cleaner "reflect" direction. But the right diagram shows the catch: the three directions are almost parallel, so very little is left after the removal.

What happened. Keeping the length changed nothing (orange dots on top of the blue ones). Removing "stop answering" removed the philosophy too (green): nothing at α 0.5, and at α 1.0 the answer breaks without becoming philosophical.

What it means. In mean-difference data, "be philosophical" and "stop answering" are the same arrow. No change of shape separates them. That leaves the two things that did keep helpfulness: asking (chapter 11) and a trained update whose push depends on the token (chapter 13). Source: FINDINGS.md § 14, runs/geometry/.

Chapter 18 · experiment J

A better teacher, a better student?

Why we tried it. In chapter 13 the student, a model trained to act prompted without the prompt, matched its teacher but couldn't beat it. So the obvious next step was a better teacher. We tried two ways, using only training questions, never the evaluation prompts.

A better prompt. Six system prompts were tested and the best on philosophy + helpfulness was kept. That was "open with one reflective sentence, then answer fully", which is less philosophical than the woven prompt but more helpful.

Better answers. We sampled four answers per question with that prompt, had the judge score all 512, and trained only on the best answer for each question. This is called best-of-n or rejection sampling.

What happened. Each student lands next to its own prompt (the arrows), not next to the hand-picked answers (the square). The picked answers were only slightly better than a typical sample, about as much as the judge's own noise, so most of what selection kept was luck the student can't learn.

What it means. Prompts and students all sit on one line (dashed): philosophy plus helpfulness of about 9.3. Choosing a teacher only slides you along it, woven toward philosophy and reflect-first toward help. Steering sits well below that line. Getting past it would need a stronger learning signal, such as preference training on judged pairs or many more samples. Source: FINDINGS.md § 15, runs/teacher/.

Chapter 19 · experiment I

Does it hold on new prompts, with a second judge?

Why we tried it. Every chapter so far scored the same 16 prompts with one judge, Claude Sonnet. Some of those prompts helped us choose settings, and one judge's habits could favour one style. So we took the main candidates to 48 new prompts that no experiment had ever used, and had a second, stronger model (Claude Opus) score everything again without seeing the first scores.

What happened. The picture holds. Each dot is Sonnet's score, and its arrow shows where Opus put the same answers. Opus is stricter about helpfulness for reflective answers, so most arrows point left, but the order doesn't change. Each trained student sits next to its prompt. At the same level of philosophy, the rank-1 student keeps about 1.7 more helpfulness points than steering under both judges. With 48 prompts that gap is about five times the uncertainty. The two judges agree item by item (correlation 0.85 for philosophy, 0.90 for helpfulness).

What it means. The best prompt-free "philosophy Qwen" we have is a rank-1 update to one matrix at layer 8, trained to imitate a prompt. It behaves like the prompted model and clearly beats steering. Chapter 20 · experiment K

Steering where the prompt flows

Why we tried it. Chapter 16 found the prompt's hot spots: three attention heads in layers 11 and 15, and the DeltaNet layers right after them. Papers on steering personas report that pushing a few heads costs less than pushing the whole hidden state. So we measured what the prompt adds at each hot spot, on average, and added that average there with no prompt.

What happened. At the hot spots, almost nothing happens, even at 4–8× the measured size (bars). Each head's contribution depends on the token: sometimes it adds one thing, sometimes the opposite. So the average is tiny and carries little of the prompt's effect. Only adding the average at all 32 token-mixing layers, doubled, made the answers philosophical, and it lands on roughly the same trade-off line as ordinary steering (chart).

What it means. Knowing where the prompt's information flows doesn't give you a vector to inject, because what flows there depends on the token. That's exactly what a constant push can't copy and what the trained update of chapter 13 can. Source: FINDINGS.md § 17, runs/heads/.

Chapter 21 · baking experiment D

Back to chapter 6: one weight edit after all

Why we tried it. The rank-1 LoRA from chapter 13 adds B × (A·a) to layer 8's output. That is exactly what you get by editing the weight once: W_down ← W_down + B·Aᵀ, a rank-1 outer product with no bias. It's the same form as the Astra-style edits of chapter 6, which barely did anything. So we merged it into the stored weights, removed every hook and prompt, and checked the model still behaves.

What happened. It does: philosophy 2.62, coherence 8.44, helpfulness 6.75, the same as the adapter within judge noise. A third of the answers are word-for-word identical. The rest drift slightly because the merged weight is stored with less precision.

So why did chapter 6's edits fail? Look at the read side, the row vᵀ (here A) that decides how big the change is on each token. The hand-built edits read from a few chosen neurons, which stay silent on "how do I fix a drain?", so there was nothing to multiply. The trained edit reads a mix of all 9,216 neurons that is active on every prompt, strongest at the start of each answer. Chapter 6's conclusion only holds for hand-picked read sides. With a learned read side, a single weight edit and no bias makes the model philosophical.

What it means. The prompt-free philosophical Qwen is the original model plus one rank-1 edit to one matrix, about 0.0003% of its weights. Source: FINDINGS.md § 18, scripts/bake_lora.py.

Glossary

Terms

Hidden state / residual stream
The 2,560 numbers per token that every layer reads and adds to; a running sum.
MLP neuron
One of 9,216 units per layer. Its activation aₙ is the read side; its column W_down[:, n] is the write side.
W_down / down_proj
The 2560×9216 matrix that turns neuron activations back into a change to the hidden state. It has no bias.
Diff-means direction
The average, over the 32 contrast pairs, of (philosophical answer's mean hidden state − plain answer's), at one layer. A single vector.
p50
The median length of the hidden state at a layer, measured on the plain answers. It sets the scale of a push.
α
Push size as a fraction of p50.
Dose
The size and sign of one neuron's push: we add dose × p50 × unit(column).
Cosine
Alignment between two vectors, ignoring length: 1 means the same direction, 0 means perpendicular.
t-statistic, consistency
Read-side statistics: how much more a neuron fires on philosophical answers than on plain ones, relative to the noise, and in what share of pairs.
OMP (orthogonal matching pursuit)
A greedy method that picks k columns and refits their weights with least squares to approximate a target vector.
÷cos² rescale
Corrects the OMP undershoot, so the push along the direction equals α.
LoRA
A trainable low-rank correction B·A added to a frozen weight matrix. Rank 1 means one read vector A and one write vector B.
Context distillation
Training a model to behave, without a prompt, the way it behaves with one, using its own prompted answers as targets.
Gated DeltaNet
The token-mixing layer used in 24 of Qwen3.5-4B's 32 layers. Instead of attending to every earlier token, it keeps a running memory (state) that each token updates and reads.
Mediation (activation patching)
Undo one part of the model's computation, by setting it to its value in another run, and measure how much of an effect disappears.
Matched random control
Random neurons in the same layer, the same number of them, the same |doses| with random signs. It separates "these neurons" from "any push".
Outer product u·vᵀ
Column times row; a rank-1 matrix whose every column is a multiple of u.
Masked rank-1 edit
Adding u·vᵀ to W_down with v nonzero only on chosen neurons. Its effect is proportional to what those neurons are doing.
Bias
The constant b in y = W·x + b; a fixed push regardless of input. Qwen3.5's text MLPs have none.
Degenerate
The judge's flag for loops, gibberish or identity confusion.
Natural change
What differs inside the model between a philosophical answer and its plain twin, with no push. It is the reference for whether a push "follows the model's own route".
Off-pathway disturbance
The part of a push's downstream effect that points away from the natural change, measured in units of the natural change.
Split-half agreement
The natural change from one half of the pairs compared with the other half. Noise keeps it below 1, so it is roughly the best score any push could get.
Helpfulness ceiling
How helpful the target answers themselves are. Ours scored 2.31 out of 9, because the philosophical answers replace the practical one.
